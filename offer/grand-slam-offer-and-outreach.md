# WHY THIS WORKS

## Step 1 — Narrow the market

**Independent specialty coffee shops, 1–3 sites, UK, owner-operator on the floor,
already running a paper stamp card.**

Against Hormozi's four criteria:

- **Pain.** Sharp and current. They are already spending money printing stamp
  cards, which means they have already admitted the problem out loud and bought a
  bad solution for it. That is the single strongest buying signal in your whole
  audience. Compare a barbershop, where loyalty is mostly personal relationship
  with one barber, so the pain is duller.
- **Purchasing power.** Enough, not abundant. A shop turning £8–12k a month can
  find £124 without a board meeting, and the owner signs on the spot. This is why
  the price ceiling in this niche is real and why the offer leans on a launch fee
  plus subscription instead of a large one-off.
- **Easy to target.** The easiest in your list. They cluster on the same three
  streets in every town, the owner is behind the counter at 10am, they run an
  active Instagram themselves, and you can walk in and buy a coffee from your
  prospect. No gatekeeper exists.
- **Growing.** Yes. UK independent coffee has kept opening sites through a period
  that closed pubs and casual-dining chains, and every one of those new shops
  opens needing regulars from a standing start.

**Why not "cafés, salons, barbers, clubs and small restaurants" together:** those
five have different visit frequencies, different staff structures and different
words for the same problem. One message cannot open all five. Coffee has the
highest natural visit frequency of the set, which is what makes a points scheme
mathematically work at all. Win coffee, get the case studies, then port the exact
same platform row to barbershops with new words.

## Step 2 — Dream outcome

In their words, not yours:

> **"I want to know my regulars by name, know when one of them stops coming, and
> be the shop people are loyal to instead of the one they walk past when the
> queue looks long."**

The status underneath it: being a local institution rather than a coffee vendor.
Owners in this niche did not open a shop to run a business, they opened it to be
somebody's morning. The offer sells belonging, and the data is how you deliver it.

Notice what is *not* the dream outcome: "a loyalty app," "customer analytics," "a
CRM." Those are features and they are what every competitor leads with.

## Step 3 — The problems

Twenty obstacles, mapped to the four terms of the Value Equation.

**Time delay**
1. Paper cards take months to reveal any pattern, and then reveal nothing.
2. Any software they have looked at needs setup hours they do not have in a
   thirteen-hour trading day.
3. Getting something custom built means months and thousands of pounds.
4. They cannot tell whether last month's promotion worked until the accountant
   tells them, long after it mattered.
5. A new scheme takes weeks to reach enough members to be worth reading.

**Effort and sacrifice**
6. No appetite for learning a dashboard between 7am and 4pm.
7. Barista turnover is constant, so anything requiring training dies by week three.
8. They have no email list and no idea how to start one legally.
9. Writing promotional copy is not their skill and they know it.
10. Anything that adds a step at the till threatens the morning queue, and they
    will not risk the queue for a marketing idea.
11. Persuading customers to download an app is a non-starter and they have already
    watched someone else fail at it.

**Perceived likelihood of achievement**
12. The loyalty apps they have seen look generic and cheap, and made the shop look
    cheaper by association.
13. They have been burned before by a web person or agency who took a deposit and
    went quiet.
14. No evidence a digital card gets used more than the paper one.
15. They do not believe their own customers would bother signing up.
16. Costa and Pret have slick apps, so they have quietly concluded this is not a
    game independents can play.
17. **You have no case studies yet.** This is your problem, but it lands as their
    risk, and it is the largest single obstacle in the list.

**Risk of failure and cost**
18. Twelve-month software contracts for a tool they may abandon in six weeks.
19. The money is competing with a new grinder, which is tangible and definitely works.
20. GDPR nerves about holding customer names and emails.
21. Fear that the list they build is hostage: stop paying, lose the customers.
22. Suspicion that discounting regulars just donates margin to people who were
    coming anyway.

**Cut in the trim (high cost to you, low value to them):**

- Native iOS/Android apps. Expensive to build and maintain, and customers will not
  download them. The QR web card is *better* for the client, not a compromise.
- Weekly or fortnightly strategy calls. Very high cost to you, and the owner does
  not want a meeting. The monthly scorecard does the job at a fraction of the cost.
- On-site staff training per hire. Does not scale past your third client.
- Bespoke campaign copy per client per month. Templates plus their shop name get
  95% of the result for 10% of the effort.
- POS integration with every till system in Britain. Enormous engineering cost,
  and the standalone tablet tool sidesteps it entirely.

**Kept (low cost to you, high value to them):** everything in the offer sheet.
Every item is either configuration, a build-once asset, or a contract term.

## Step 7 — Maximising the Value Equation

$$\text{Value} = \frac{\text{Dream Outcome} \times \text{Perceived Likelihood of Achievement}}{\text{Time Delay} \times \text{Effort and Sacrifice}}$$

- **Dream outcome, increased.** The promise is not software access, it is 150
  named regulars and a shop that looks like it has its own club. You raise the
  ceiling further by making the club carry their brand, so the outcome includes
  status, not just revenue.
- **Perceived likelihood, increased.** Two guarantees, a specific number rather
  than a vague improvement, list export so nothing is hostage, a printed kit that
  makes enrolment visibly inevitable, and the win-back campaigns run by you rather
  than by them. You cannot yet raise this with case studies, so you raise it by
  absorbing the risk.
- **Time delay, decreased.** Live in 7 days from receiving the logo. First member
  enrolled the day it goes live. First scorecard inside 30 days. Compare a custom
  build at three months, or a self-serve tool that sits unconfigured forever.
- **Effort and sacrifice, decreased.** The owner's total workload is: send a logo,
  answer eleven questions, put a card on each table. No setup, no copywriting, no
  dashboard, no app to sell to customers, no till change beyond one tap.

**Where you win on each term:** Loyalzoo and Stamp Me compete on the denominator
only, and only halfway, by being cheap and quick to switch on. They lose the
numerator entirely: generic branding caps the dream outcome, and self-serve means
the owner supplies the likelihood themselves. Your whole edge is that you take the
numerator seriously at their denominator.

## Step 8 — The price

**Anchor: £1,497 for the first year, paid up front.**
**Payment plan: £297 launch, then £147 a month, three-month minimum.**

The reasoning:

- **The value-to-price gap.** Deliverables and bonuses stack to £1,850 before the
  platform itself is counted. Priced at £1,497 for a full year, the client is
  reading a number below the value of the extras alone.
- **Their realistic ROI.** Take 150 members averaging four visits a month at
  £4.20. That is £2,520 of monthly member spend. A conservative 15% frequency lift
  is roughly £378 in extra monthly revenue, and at coffee's gross margin that is
  around £265 a month of gross profit. Against £124 a month, that is a little over
  2x, before win-backs and before the review flow brings anyone new. Say 2–3x and
  you are safe. **Do not promise more than this.** The guarantee is what sells this,
  not an inflated projection.
- **Why competing on price loses here.** At £39 you are cheaper than Loyalzoo and
  you have destroyed the entire pitch, because "looks custom-built" is not
  believable at pocket-money prices. Your product's one advantage is that it does
  not look like software, and price is the loudest signal of that. You also cannot
  afford it: the offer includes human time on branding, print and monthly
  campaigns, so a £39 client is a client you lose money serving by month four.
  Your marginal platform cost is near zero, which means the correct strategy is
  premium price and low volume, not the reverse.
- **Why the annual is the recommended anchor.** It funds the print kit and your
  setup time on day one, it removes month-to-month churn during the exact window
  where the client has not yet seen results, and it makes the guarantee affordable
  to honour. The monthly plan exists to be the more expensive option that makes
  the annual obvious. Year one monthly totals £2,061 against £1,497.

## Step 10 — Guarantees

**Unconditional, 30 days:**

> "For 30 days from go-live, if you want it gone, say so and I refund every penny.
> You keep your exported customer list. No forms, no conversation about why."

*Risk to you in plain terms:* you lose 4–6 hours of setup and roughly £40 of
print. Your platform cost for one more row in a database is pennies. You can
honour this indefinitely. The real exposure is opportunity cost, not cash.

**Conditional, performance-based:**

> "If your club has not reached 150 enrolled members within 90 days of go-live, I
> will continue running your campaigns at no charge until it does, and I will
> refund your launch fee in full. You keep the platform and your customer list
> throughout."

*Risk to you in plain terms:* the failure mode is a shop that never puts the table
talkers out, and you have then promised unpaid work indefinitely. **Cap it:** make
the guarantee conditional on the client displaying the supplied kit and their
staff awarding points on at least 60% of trading days, both of which your own
dashboard can verify. Word the condition into the agreement, not into the offer
sheet. Also cap the free-work period at 90 further days, then convert to a full
refund and part ways. Without those two caps this guarantee is genuinely dangerous
to you.

Lead with the performance guarantee in outreach. It is the only thing you have
that substitutes for case studies.

## Step 11 — Scarcity and urgency

Both built from real constraints, nothing invented:

- **Real capacity limit.** Four onboardings a month, because each includes hand-built

  understands exactly what they are trading.

Do not run expiring discount timers. In a niche this small and this
word-of-mouth-driven, being caught with a permanent "ends Friday" costs you more
than the urgency earns.

## The weakest part of the offer

**Perceived likelihood of achievement, and specifically the absence of one real
shop with real numbers.** A £1,497 upfront ask from a stranger with no case study
is the single largest gap between this offer and its conversion ceiling. The double
guarantee is the correct patch, but a guarantee makes a sceptic hesitate where a
neighbour's numbers make them act, and in a niche where every owner knows every
other owner, that gap is unusually expensive.

Second weakest: the performance guarantee is uncapped as written on the sheet.
Apply the two caps in step 10 before you send this to anyone.

## The single change that would most improve conversion

**Get one shop live for free, this month, in exchange for their numbers and their
name.**

Pick the busiest independent coffee shop within walking distance whose owner you
can actually meet. Give them the whole thing at no cost for 90 days, in writing,
in exchange for permission to publish their results and one filmed sentence from
the owner. Then rewrite the opening line of every message in this document from a
claim into a fact:

> "The Bear on Fore Street added 187 members in nine weeks and 22% of them now
> come in more often. Same thing, your logo, your shop."

That one line does more for conversion than any change to the price, the bonuses
or the guarantee, because it converts your only real weakness into your only real
proof. Everything else in this offer is already built to hold. The cost is one
month of forgone revenue from a single client you do not yet have, which is the
cheapest thing you will ever buy.

Do this before you send a single cold email. It is worth the delay.
